# Expired Domains: Why Half the PBN World Runs on Them (And When That Is Fine)

Ask where PBN domains come from and the honest answer is: most of them used to be somebody else's website. A bakery in Ohio closes, its domain registration lapses, and a network builder buys it at auction — not for the name, but for the ten years of links the bakery collected without ever thinking about SEO. That is an expired domain, and it is the raw material of most of the PBN industry.

**Why buyers of domains want them.** A brand-new domain has no links, no history and no authority; Google treats it accordingly. An expired domain arrives with all three already attached. Rebuilt as a blog in a similar niche, it can pass real value from day one — value that would take years and far more money to build from zero.

**When it is fine.** The domain's old life was clean and reasonably relevant, its existing links come from real sites, and its archive shows a normal website rather than a spam episode. Most expired domains traded by serious networks pass these checks, because the network's own product dies if they do not.

**When it is not fine.** Three histories should kill a purchase instantly: the domain was previously used for spam or doorway pages; it was burned by a penalty and dumped; or it has been re-registered and re-purposed five times in five niches. All three are visible in the domain's archive and link profile to anyone who spends four minutes looking — which is precisely why honest sellers let you filter and inspect inventory instead of selling you a sealed mystery bag.

**The buyer's takeaway.** Expired domains are not a trick; they are a shortcut with a paper trail. Judge the paper trail and the shortcut is sound.
